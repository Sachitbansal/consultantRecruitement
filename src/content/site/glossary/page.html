Understand the Language of Growth and Value Creation.

Clear explanations of business, operating and investment-readiness concepts for promoters, leadership teams and investors.

By GOP Editorial · Published 24 October 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

What is this glossary? Plain-language definitions of eighteen terms promoters, leadership teams and investors meet when they plan growth, investment readiness and value creation. Each entry says what the term means, why it matters and, where useful, gives an example, with a link to a GOP page that goes deeper.

Definitions here are general explanations. They are not professional advice; how a term applies to a specific business depends on its context.

Profitable Growth · EBITDA · Working Capital · Commercial Excellence · Customer Acquisition Cost · Customer Lifetime Value · Gross Margin · Operating Leverage · Investment Readiness · Value-Creation Plan · Portfolio Company · Due Diligence · Capital Allocation · Governance · Management Reporting · Market Expansion · Sales Productivity · Post-Investment Value Creation

Profitable Growth

Revenue growth that also raises profit, cash and the quality of earnings, rather than growth bought with deeper discounts, longer credit or rising cost.

Why it matters: Growth that dilutes margin can make a business bigger and weaker at the same time. Investors look at whether growth pays for itself.

Example: Winning a new segment at list price with normal credit is profitable growth; winning the same volume at a 15% discount and 120-day credit may not be.

Read more: Profitable growth and market expansion.

EBITDA

Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation: operating profit before financing costs and non-cash charges.

Why it matters: It is the most common starting point for valuing a mid-size business, so how it is calculated, and which adjustments are made, matter in any investor discussion.

Example: A one-off gain on selling land should not be counted in the EBITDA an investor values the business on.

Read more: QoE-grade reporting basics.

Working Capital

The money tied up in running the business day to day: inventory plus receivables minus payables.

Why it matters: A growing business can run out of cash while making a profit, because each rupee of extra sales ties up more stock and receivables.

Read more: Working capital discipline.

Commercial Excellence

The discipline of earning the most from the business you already have: pricing, discount control, key-account management and an effective sales process.

Why it matters: It is often the fastest visible return, because the margin is already inside the order book waiting to be recovered.

Read more: Commercial excellence and brand growth.

Customer Acquisition Cost

The total sales and marketing spend needed to win one new customer, over a defined period.

Why it matters: Spending on demand only makes sense when acquisition cost is low enough relative to the margin a customer brings.

Example: If a campaign costs INR 10 lakh and wins five customers, acquisition cost is INR 2 lakh per customer.

Read more: Ad spend versus unit economics.

Customer Lifetime Value

The total margin a customer is expected to contribute over the whole relationship, not just the first order.

Why it matters: Compared with acquisition cost, it shows which customers and channels are worth investing in.

Read more: Ad spend versus unit economics.

Gross Margin

Revenue minus the direct cost of the goods sold, as a share of revenue.

Why it matters: A blended gross margin can hide products and customers that lose money once freight, credit and returns are counted.

Read more: Costing architecture.

Operating Leverage

The degree to which profit grows faster than revenue because part of the cost base is fixed.

Why it matters: High operating leverage rewards growth and punishes decline, so capacity and cost decisions should reflect both.

Example: A plant running at 60% of capacity can add volume with little extra fixed cost, so each extra sale contributes more profit.

Read more: Operational excellence and margin improvement.

Investment Readiness

The state in which an outside investor can trust a business's numbers, people, governance and plan without relying on the promoter's memory.

Why it matters: Readiness does not guarantee investment, but its absence lowers valuations and slows or stops deals.

Read more: Governance and investment readiness.

Value-Creation Plan

A prioritised, owned and measured set of initiatives that turns an investment thesis into operating results.

Why it matters: It is the link between what an investor underwrote and what the leadership team does on Monday morning.

Read more: Growth capital and post-investment value creation.

Portfolio Company

A business in which a private equity or venture capital fund has invested.

Why it matters: Funds expect portfolio companies to deliver against the investment thesis, often with operating support beyond the capital itself.

Read more: For PE/VC funds.

Due Diligence

The investigation an investor or acquirer carries out before committing capital, covering financial, commercial, operational, legal and tax matters.

Why it matters: Gaps found by the investor cost more than gaps found and fixed by the business first.

Read more: What investors diligence.

Capital Allocation

How a business decides where to spend its money: capacity, working capital, new markets, debt reduction or returns to owners.

Why it matters: Investors judge management partly on the discipline of these decisions and on the returns they produce.

Read more: Growth capital.

Governance

The structures and routines that decide who can decide what: board calendar, committees, delegation of authority and approval limits.

Why it matters: Good governance lets a business keep its rhythm without depending on one person, which is what investors look for.

Read more: Governance and MIS.

Management Reporting

The regular pack of operating and financial information leadership uses to run the business, often called MIS.

Why it matters: If it arrives late or is argued over, decisions are made on instinct. Investors read its quality as a signal of management quality.

Read more: MIS design.

Market Expansion

Growing into new customer segments, products, categories, channels or geographies.

Why it matters: Expansion that is prioritised on evidence about margin and capability is far more likely to pay off than expansion that follows competitors.

Read more: Profitable growth and market expansion.

Sales Productivity

The revenue or margin produced per unit of sales effort, such as per salesperson, per call or per rupee of sales cost.

Why it matters: It shows whether adding sales capacity will add revenue, or whether the process needs fixing first.

Read more: Commercial excellence.

Post-Investment Value Creation

The work done after capital arrives to deliver the growth, margin and capability changes the investment assumed.

Why it matters: The first year after investment often decides whether the thesis is delivered. It needs a plan, owners and a shared scoreboard from day one.

Read more: Growth capital and post-investment value creation.
