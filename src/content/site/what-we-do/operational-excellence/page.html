Operational Excellence & Cost Optimization

Lever 1 of GOP's Five Levers. How do manufacturers cut cost and raise capacity without new capital?

By GOP Editorial · Published 23 October 2026 · Updated 23 October 2026

How do manufacturers cut cost and raise capacity without new capital? GOP embeds senior operators on your shop floor and in your supply chain to lift output, cut waste and lower cost per unit, often within existing space and assets. The work is measured in capacity, throughput, productivity and landed cost, not in recommendations.

The problem this lever solves

Many promoter-led manufacturers in the INR 150-500 crore range have outgrown the way their plants were set up. Layouts grew by addition, batches are large because changeovers are slow, and material moves further than it needs to. The result is a plant that looks busy but ships less than it should, with cost creeping up each year.

The usual response is to buy machines or add shifts. In most plants the faster and cheaper gain is in flow: removing waiting, rework, searching, long changeovers and unnecessary handling. That is shop-floor work, and it needs people who have run plants, not only analysed them.

Five interventions

a. Flow, takt and pull redesign

We map value flow from raw material to dispatch, set takt time to real demand, and redesign layout and sequencing so work is pulled by the next process instead of pushed in batches. In earlier engagements delivered by our practice leads, this doubled installed capacity in heat exchangers with no additional floor space.

b. Changeover, throughput and productivity programmes

We time every step of changeovers, move preparation outside machine stoppage and standardise the routine. In one press shop, changeover fell from 70 to 40 minutes, a 45% reduction, directly raising available production hours.

c. Plant layout and materials-handling redesign

We redesign layouts and conveyorised flow to cut walking, handling and waiting. A materials-handling redesign brought one plant live with 60% manpower optimisation, and a new fan plant planned for 6 lakh units a month was built in 40% less space than conventional design.

d. Procurement and vendor cost rationalisation

We analyse spend by category, rationalise vendors, standardise specifications and negotiate on total landed cost rather than unit price alone.

e. Supplier quality and delivery improvement

We assess key suppliers against quality and delivery criteria, close gaps with them on the ground and track supplier performance on a scoreboard your purchasing team owns.

AI deployment from sourcing to dispatch: demand-linked planning, supplier and inventory visibility, and exception alerts that move decisions from monthly reviews to the daily routine.

What changes in the first 90 days

  • Days 1-30: Baseline capacity, changeover times, line balance, material flow and landed cost. Rank the opportunities by gain and effort.
  • Days 31-60: Implement the first flow and changeover changes on one or two lines. Install a daily management board with capacity and quality KPIs.
  • Days 61-90: Extend to remaining lines, lock in standard work, and hand the scoreboard to plant owners.

How progress is measured

  • Installed and effective capacity per line
  • Changeover time (average and worst case)
  • Manpower per unit and productivity
  • Floor space per unit of output
  • Landed cost per unit
  • Supplier on-time delivery and quality

Who this lever is for

This lever suits promoter-led manufacturers with ongoing growth pressure on capacity or margin: fans and motors, home appliances, electricals and switchgear, heat exchangers, engineering components, paper and packaging, FMCG and consumer durables.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Buying machines before measuring losses on existing ones.
  • Improving a single process and moving the bottleneck instead of raising output.
  • Running large batches to avoid changeovers.
  • Treating improvement as a project instead of a daily routine.

Questions to ask yourself before starting

  • Do we know our changeover times and our line balance?
  • Is our floor space used for work in process rather than value-adding work?
  • Does a daily routine keep improvements in place?

A hypothetical scenario

Hypothetical, for illustration only. Not a client case. A fabrication unit runs at a quarter below its planned output while orders back up. A diagnostic finds long changeovers, material waiting between stations and a layout that forces long moves. Changing sequence, preparing changeovers outside machine stoppage and installing a daily line board lifts output on the same equipment, so the planned machine purchase is deferred.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need new machinery to raise capacity?

Often not. Most early gains come from flow, changeover and layout. New machinery is recommended only after the existing assets are running at their potential.

How fast do results show?

The first measurable changes usually appear within weeks on the lines chosen first. Broader gains build over the following quarter as the routines spread.

Do operators replace our plant team?

No. Operators work with your managers and supervisors, teach the methods on live lines and hand over the routines so the gains stay.
