Commercial Excellence & Value Capturing

Lever 2 of GOP's Five Levers. How do manufacturers earn more from the business they already have?

By GOP Editorial · Published 23 October 2026 · Updated 23 October 2026

How do manufacturers earn more from the business they already have? GOP fixes pricing, margin, key-account and sales-process leakage before any new demand is bought. The aim is to recover value that is already inside the current order book, then build the routines that stop it leaking again: true profitability by customer and product, discount bands with approval limits, key-account plans and a weekly scoreboard with an owner for every metric.

The problem this lever solves

In many promoter-led manufacturers, the promoter has personally negotiated the big accounts for years. Prices, discounts and terms live in relationships and memory. Nobody has a clean view of which customers and products actually make money after freight, credit, returns and service.

Spending on new demand before this is fixed multiplies the leak: new orders arrive on the same weak economics. Commercial excellence is therefore the fastest visible return in most mandates, because the margin is already there to be recovered.

Five interventions

a. Customer and SKU profitability analysis

We build a true-economics view by customer, product and channel, including freight, credit period, rebates and returns, so the real margin picture replaces the blended average.

b. Pricing and discount discipline

We compare list price with realised price, set discount bands by volume and customer type, and define an escalation path for exceptions, so concessions become decisions instead of habits.

c. Key-account planning and sales-process redesign

We structure account plans for the customers that matter, define roles and cadence, and redesign the process from enquiry to order so follow-up does not depend on memory.

d. Channel and distributor performance management

We set targets, margins and service standards for distributors and channel partners, and review them against a common scorecard.

e. Weekly KPI scoreboard with an owner for every metric

We install a simple weekly scoreboard covering price realisation, margin, order intake, collections and pipeline, each metric with a named owner and a decision rhythm.

AI deployment from enquiry to cash: quote support, margin alerts at order entry, and receivable follow-up that reduces the time between order and payment.

What changes in the first 90 days

  • Days 1-30: Build the customer and SKU profitability view. Identify the biggest sources of leakage.
  • Days 31-60: Introduce discount rules and approval limits. Start account plans for the top customers.
  • Days 61-90: Run the weekly scoreboard, review results with the promoter and sales leaders, and adjust.

How progress is measured

  • Price realisation against list
  • Gross margin by customer and product
  • Discount as a share of sales
  • Order intake and win rate
  • Days sales outstanding
  • Share of revenue from top accounts

Who this lever is for

This lever suits manufacturers with strong products and long customer relationships but unclear margin by account, high discount dependence or sales that rely on the promoter.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Giving similar discounts to every large customer regardless of cost to serve.
  • Letting exceptions be approved by the person who benefits from the order.
  • Looking at blended margin instead of margin by customer.
  • Adding sales capacity before fixing the sales process.

Questions to ask yourself before starting

  • Do we know profit by customer and product after freight, credit and returns?
  • Who approves discount exceptions?
  • Do sales and finance see the same numbers?

What good looks like after a year

The promoter no longer negotiates every large order personally, because the sales team works inside agreed bands and knows when to escalate. Finance and sales argue less, because both read the same profitability view. Discount exceptions are rare enough to be discussed by name in the weekly review. Collections are part of the sales conversation, not a separate chase by accounts. And when the business does start spending on new demand, it does so knowing which products and customers it actually wants more of.

A hypothetical scenario

Hypothetical, for illustration only. Not a client case. A manufacturer sees margin slip while volumes rise. A profitability review shows that a handful of large accounts take deep discounts and long credit, and that several products earn little once freight and returns are counted. The sales team assumed every one of those concessions was needed to keep the business. New discount bands, an approval path for exceptions and a weekly scoreboard are introduced, and the largest accounts are approached with data on cost to serve rather than with a demand. Margin is recovered from the existing order book before any money is spent on new demand.

Frequently asked questions

Will tighter discount rules lose us customers?

Rules are set with data on which discounted deals were genuinely at risk. Most firms find that fewer deals needed the concession than the sales team assumed.

Do we need new software?

Not to begin. A clean spreadsheet-based profitability view and a weekly scoreboard come first. Systems follow once the process is proven.

How is this different from hiring a sales head?

A sales head runs a function. This lever fixes the commercial system across pricing, accounts and process, and develops your leaders to run it.
